The Return of the Urban Orchard

AIn the decades after the Second World War, fruit trees quietly disappeared from many European and North American cities. Orchards that had once supplied neighbourhood markets were cleared for housing, and the few remaining trees in parks were often removed because fallen fruit was regarded as a nuisance rather than a resource.

BSince the early 2000s, however, a number of community groups have begun to replant. In some cities, volunteers map every publicly accessible fruit tree and publish the locations online, inviting residents to harvest what would otherwise rot. Organisers argue that these maps do more than reduce waste: they encourage people to walk through parts of the city they would not normally visit.

CMunicipal authorities have responded with caution. Maintenance is the most frequently cited concern. Unlike ornamental trees, fruit trees require regular pruning, and a poorly maintained tree can drop heavy branches onto paths. Several councils now require community groups to sign agreements committing volunteers to annual careghi chú before any planting is approved.

DThere are also questions about safety. Early critics suggested that fruit grown beside busy roads might absorb dangerous levels of pollution. A three-year study in one northern city found that, once washed, most fruit contained lead concentrations well below legal limits, although the researchers recommended that trees not be planted within ten metres of major roads.

ESupporters point to benefits that are harder to measure. Teachers report that pupils who help to harvest school orchards become more willing to try unfamiliar fruit, and some hospitals have planted small orchards in the belief that green spaces aid recovery.

FWhether urban orchards can make a meaningful contribution to food supply remains doubtful. Even the most ambitious projects produce only a fraction of what a city consumes. Their real value, many organisers concede, may lie in reconnecting residents with the seasons.
